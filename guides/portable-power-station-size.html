What size portable power station do you need?

Updated November 1, 2026 · 5 min read

Disclosure: we earn a commission on qualifying purchases made through links on this site. Details.

Two numbers decide whether a power station will work for you: how much energy it stores, and how much power it can deliver at once. Mixing them up is the most common buying mistake.

Watt-hours vs. watts

Watt-hours (Wh) is the size of the tank. Watts (W) is how fast you can draw from it. A 1,000 Wh unit with a 1,000 W inverter can run a 100 W device for roughly ten hours in theory. Real-world runtime is lower because the inverter wastes some energy, so plan on getting about 80-85% of the rated capacity.

The sizing formula

  1. List what you want to keep running.
  2. For each, find its watts (the label, the manual, or a plug-in power meter).
  3. Multiply watts by hours of use per day to get Wh per day.
  4. Add them up, multiply by the days you want to cover, then add about 15-20% for losses.
  5. Check that the sum of what runs at the same moment is below the inverter's rated watts.

Rough size tiers

  • 300-600 Wh: phones, Wi-Fi, a few lights, a CPAP overnight.
  • 1,000-1,500 Wh: the above plus a refrigerator for part of a day.
  • 2,000-4,000 Wh: a fridge and essentials for a day or more, or longer with solar recharging.
  • Beyond that: you are into installed battery or generator territory.

Things that catch people out

  • Startup surge. Motors, like fridge compressors and pumps, briefly draw several times their running watts. Check the unit's surge rating.
  • Heating appliances. Space heaters, kettles, toasters and hair dryers use huge power for what they do. A battery is the wrong tool for electric heat.
  • Cold weather. Lithium batteries lose capacity and may refuse to charge when very cold. Keep the unit indoors and check the manufacturer's temperature range.
  • Recharging. Ask how you will refill it in a multi-day outage: solar input, car charging, or a generator.
